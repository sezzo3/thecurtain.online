After Eight Years, a Brooklyn Jury Finds Huawei Guilty of Fraud and Trade-Secret Theft — but Not Racketeering

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-25 · 5 min read

A federal jury in Brooklyn convicted Huawei Technologies and three of its subsidiaries on Tuesday of bank fraud, wire fraud, sanctions violations and conspiring to steal trade secrets, closing an eight-year criminal case that has shadowed relations between the United States and China since 2018. The jury found the companies guilty on 10 of the 12 criminal counts they faced, including conspiracy to steal trade secrets, bank fraud conspiracy, conspiracy to defraud the United States, violations of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and money laundering conspiracy. But it acquitted Huawei of the two most sweeping charges — racketeering conspiracy, which alleged the company operated as a criminal enterprise, and conspiracy to obstruct justice — a result the company's lawyer called a "profound rejection" of the government's portrait of Huawei as corrupt from top to bottom.

The trial, which lasted eleven to twelve weeks before United States District Judge Ann M. Donnelly, heard from more than 60 prosecution witnesses and 15 for the defence. Prosecutors told jurors that Huawei had deceived banks including HSBC, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas about its business in Iran, so that it could obtain billions of dollars in credit and access to United States dollar-clearing services while moving money in and out of Iran in violation of American sanctions. They also accused the company of a two-decade campaign to steal intellectual property from American firms: source code from Cisco Systems for its routers, antenna technology from the wireless company Quintel, access to a circuit board developed by the semiconductor company CNEX through a hired professor, and technical data about T-Mobile's testing robot, known as Tappy — an employee, prosecutors said, photographed the robot and slipped a piece of it into a backpack. "Committing crimes to get ahead was ingrained in the very culture of Huawei," Assistant United States Attorney Meredith Arfa told jurors in closing arguments, saying employees had bragged about the thefts on an informal company forum.

Huawei's defence argued throughout that prosecutors were criminalising ordinary business activity, that much of the allegedly stolen material was not secret, and that the banks knew about its Iran business and competed for it. After the verdict, the company's lawyer, Brian Heberlig, said Huawei was disappointed by the convictions but that the racketeering acquittal "confirms that these allegations lack factual basis and vindicates our consistent position over the past eight years." Huawei said in a statement that the conviction was "without legal or factual merit", and Heberlig told reporters: "We fully expect to appeal." He said it was premature to discuss penalties, but that the racketeering acquittal could make a significant difference to the eventual fine.

The case began with an indictment in 2018 that also secretly charged Meng Wanzhou, Huawei's chief financial officer and the daughter of its founder Ren Zhengfei, with misleading an HSBC banker about the company's Iran business. Meng was arrested in Canada in December 2018, setting off a diplomatic crisis; she returned to China in 2021 under a deferred prosecution agreement with the Justice Department. The following year, the United States placed Huawei on a trade blacklist that cut off its access to American technology, and in 2020 prosecutors added the trade-secret conspiracy charges. For eight years the prosecution has been a central front in the technology rivalry between Washington and Beijing, and one of the rare American criminal cases brought against a foreign corporation of Huawei's size.

The reaction on Tuesday split along familiar lines. United States Attorney Joseph Nocella said in a statement: "Few crimes are as serious as Huawei's decades-long pattern of endangering US national security, violating the sanctity of US commerce, and impeding the enforcement of US laws." Attorney General Todd Blanche joined in announcing the verdicts. China's government has long condemned the prosecution: its Foreign Ministry has repeatedly called it politically motivated "lawfare" and economic coercion, and spokesman Guo Jiakun said in September that "China firmly supports Chinese enterprises in safeguarding their legitimate rights and interests." China's embassy in Washington did not immediately respond to a request for comment on the verdict.

What happens next will take time. Judge Donnelly is scheduled to sentence the companies on 14 September 2027, and the Associated Press reported that Huawei could face penalties running into billions of dollars. An appeal is expected, and the size of the fine — and whether the racketeering acquittal limits it — will be fought over for months. The verdict also lands at a delicate moment for the wider relationship: a United States-China trade truce caps second-term tariffs on Chinese goods until 10 January, and Washington is preparing a new round of tariffs on sixteen trading partners. For Huawei, the company that became the symbol of China's technological rise and of Washington's determination to contain it, the jury's verdict is a legal defeat eight years in the making — but the acquittal on the racketeering charge means the government's most ambitious claim about the company did not survive the courtroom.
