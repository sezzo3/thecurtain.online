Britain's Benefits System Goes Offline as a Cyber Incident Hits the Department for Work and Pensions

Sezgin Baktiaya · 2026-11-27 · 5 min read

Britain's welfare system was partially shut down on Friday after the Department for Work and Pensions discovered a cyber incident affecting its computer systems, taking online services offline as a precaution while investigators from GCHQ's National Cyber Security Centre work to establish what happened. The department, which administers benefits and pensions to around 20 million people, said regular payments would continue as normal, but the shutdown left claimants unable to make new claims or sign in to their online accounts.

The department announced the incident on Friday morning, saying in a statement on GOV.UK that it had "taken some DWP online services offline as a precaution after we identified a cyber incident affecting our systems". The Times reported that all of the department's online services were taken down at about 9pm on Thursday in response to "suspicious activity". The department said it was "working closely with the National Cyber Security Centre and Government Cyber Coordination Centre to investigate and to bring services back online as quickly as possible and to minimise any disruption", and added: "Regular benefit and pension payments are not affected and will be paid as normal."

The outage cut off a wide range of services that millions of people rely on every day. Claimants could not apply for Universal Credit, sign in to an existing Universal Credit account, or search for a job, and online applications for Personal Independence Payment, the main disability benefit, were also unavailable. The "Tell Us Once" service, which lets families inform several government departments at once when someone dies, was taken down, as was the service for sending fit notes for Employment and Support Allowance, and State Pension services online could not be reached. With about 96,890 staff, the department is one of the largest in Whitehall, and the scale of the disruption underlines how much of the welfare state now runs through its websites.

What caused the incident remains unclear. The Times reported that it did not involve a ransomware attack and that no money had been moved, and the Press Association reported on Friday morning that there was no evidence yet that any personal data from claimants had been stolen, although investigations were continuing. The department has declined to comment beyond its public statement, has published no list of the services affected, and has given no date for when they will return. The National Cyber Security Centre said it had nothing to add to the department's statement, and the department's only advice to claimants was to try again later.

Parliament's scrutiny of the incident began quickly. Debbie Abrahams, chair of the Commons Work and Pensions Committee, said it was a "great relief" that benefit and pension payments had not been affected, adding that the department had "taken swift action to contain any potential further damage from the cyber incident and so that service is restored as soon as possible". The department also used its statement to warn claimants about fraud, saying: "As always, we will never message you to ask for bank details or ask you to click on links." Phone helplines, including the Universal Credit helpline on 0800 328 5644, remained open for people who could not wait.

The incident is the latest in a run of damaging cyber attacks on British institutions. Reuters noted that British companies and institutions have been hit by aggressive and regular cyber attacks in recent years, with some suffering months of disruption, including the British Library, a blood testing service, the London Underground, Marks & Spencer, the Co-op and Jaguar Land Rover. Earlier this month, a National Cyber Security Centre report urged organisations to strengthen their defences against hackers as the technology becomes more complex and capable, and the centre's chief executive has warned that AI-powered malware is strongly expected to feature in ransomware attacks on the UK's critical national infrastructure by 2028. For now, the department says its priority is restoring services, but until it explains what happened, claimants and MPs will be asking how the system that pays 20 million people came to be switched off overnight.
