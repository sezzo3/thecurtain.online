UK Net Migration Falls to 77,000, Its Lowest Level in Almost Three Decades

Sezgin Baktiaya · 2026-11-26 · 5 min read

Britain recorded its lowest level of annual net migration in almost three decades on Thursday, as official figures showed the number of people arriving minus those leaving fell to an estimated 77,000 in the year to June 2026 — a drop of 62 per cent in a single year and a level not seen since 1997, outside the coronavirus pandemic. The Office for National Statistics (ONS) said about 738,000 people moved to Britain in the period while 661,000 left, giving a net figure of 77,000, down from 204,000 in the previous 12 months and down 92 per cent from the peak of 944,000 in the year to March 2023. It is the level that previous governments spent more than a decade pursuing: former Conservative prime minister David Cameron first pledged in 2010 to reduce net migration to the tens of thousands, a target his successors repeatedly missed as the figure climbed towards a million.

The fall was driven by a sharp drop in arrivals rather than a surge in departures. Long-term immigration fell 17 per cent to 738,000, from 893,000 the year before, while emigration was broadly stable at 661,000, against an updated estimate of 689,000. The ONS said the continued reduction was driven by fewer people from outside the EU arriving in the UK to work or study, and that non-EU citizens are now the only group driving positive net migration. Net migration among nationals from outside the EU, Norway and Switzerland stood at 258,000, down from 379,000 a year earlier. An estimated 245,000 British nationals left the UK in the year to June 2026, slightly fewer than the 251,000 who left the year before and the 257,000 the year before that. The ONS stressed that the figures are provisional and warned that its estimates for British nationals carry greater uncertainty, because updated data was unavailable and an extended 15-month projection had to be used.

The government claimed the figures as a vindication of its approach. Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood said: "This Government has restored control. We have brought net migration to 77,000, down 92% from its peak under the Conservatives." She added: "The Conservatives lost control of our borders. Year upon year, they promised they would bring net migration down to the tens of thousands. Instead, they let it reach close to a million a year, overseeing four decades worth of migration in their last four years alone." Ms Mahmood said the fall "didn't happen by accident", pointing to raised skills and salary thresholds "to only attract the brightest and best and those we truly need", and said the government was "bearing down on illegal migration", with small boat crossings down significantly, asylum hotels being closed rapidly and people here illegally being removed. "There is more to do. But this Government is restoring order to our border and fairness to our migration system," she said.

The fall in legal migration follows restrictions imposed by both the previous Conservative government and the current Labour government. Measures under Rishi Sunak — higher salary thresholds for skilled workers and restrictions on most overseas students and care workers bringing dependants — are credited by many analysts with starting the decline, and the present government has signalled it intends to go further, earlier this month unveiling plans to double the standard wait for permanent settlement from five to ten years. Separate Home Office data for the year to September 2026 showed work visas granted down 16 per cent to 230,000, sponsored study visas down 23 per cent to 336,000, and family visas down 17 per cent to 56,000. The number of people detected arriving illegally fell to 31,077, its lowest level in five years, including 26,581 small-boat arrivals, the lowest since 2021, and 93,000 people were receiving asylum support, 16 per cent fewer than a year earlier.

The political argument over who deserves the credit began within minutes of the figures' release. Chris Philp, the shadow home secretary, said the fall was "mainly a result of changes made by the previous Conservative government" and called for a legally binding annual cap on migration, set by Parliament, "at a far lower level". He claimed that "British and European people are leaving in their droves because of Labour's high taxes whilst non-EU immigration remains far too high". Reform UK said the figures were "no cause for celebration", arguing that years of high migration under Conservative and Labour governments had strained public services and housing. Immigration remains a major political issue despite the falling numbers: a YouGov poll published this week showed it was the second-most important concern for voters after the economy, and the number of people claiming asylum was 73,000 in the period, down by more than a fifth on the previous year but still higher than levels seen between 2004 and 2021.

Beyond the politics, economists warn that the sharp fall carries risks of its own. A sustained drop in immigration would shrink Britain's workforce and weigh on its growth potential, while also straining the public finances. Oxford Economics estimated in April that if net migration fell to zero over the long term, the economy would be more than 15 per cent smaller by 2060 than in its baseline forecast, and lower tax revenue would push up government borrowing even after allowing for a smaller population needing fewer public services. The figures are also likely to have implications for employers relying on international recruitment to fill skills shortages, particularly in sectors where overseas workers have traditionally played an important role. Sunder Katwala, director of the British Future think tank, said the scale of the drop would surprise much of the public, many of whom still believed immigration was rising, and argued that politicians should now move on from debates about reducing migration and focus on what comes next. "The government needs to ask what a sustainable level of immigration is, and how will they achieve it," he said. What happens next will be shaped by the government's next round of reforms and by whether the figures hold up to revision. The ONS has changed how it measures uncertainty for this release, using revision-based intervals for most estimates, and the British-nationals figures in particular could be revised as better data arrives. Ministers have promised further changes to make the system fairer, and the settlement-waiting-time proposal unveiled earlier this month suggests the direction of travel remains towards restriction. But with net migration now at the level once promised as a target, the debate in Westminster is already shifting — from how low the number can go, to what a country with 77,000 net arrivals a year does about the workers, students and taxpayers it no longer receives.
