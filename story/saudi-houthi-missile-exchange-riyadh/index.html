Saudi Arabia Intercepts Houthi Cruise Missile Aimed at Riyadh as the Cross-Border War Escalates

Sezgin Baktiaya · 2026-11-28 · 5 min read

Saudi air defences intercepted and destroyed a Houthi cruise missile fired towards Riyadh early on Saturday, the Saudi-led coalition said, in the latest strike of a week in which missiles, drones and air raids have flown in both directions across the Saudi-Yemeni border. The coalition's spokesman, Major General Turki al-Maliki, announced the interception in a post on X, saying air defences had destroyed the cruise missile launched by what he called the Houthi terrorist militia towards the Riyadh region. He gave no details of where the missile was launched from, when it was fired, or whether it caused any damage, and no casualties were reported in connection with the interception. The coalition said on Saturday that its air defences had also intercepted a Houthi ballistic missile targeting the kingdom's southern region.

Saturday's interception was the third consecutive day of attacks on the Saudi capital, and the third distinct weapon type fired at Riyadh in 72 hours. On Thursday, a Houthi drone broke through the defences and wounded 11 people in Riyadh, a strike on the Jazan oil refinery on the Red Sea coast killed one worker, a death confirmed by the Saudi Energy Ministry, and the coalition said it had destroyed two Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at the capital. On Friday, the coalition said it had intercepted two Houthi drones targeting the Riyadh region.

The week had begun with a projectile striking the King Abdullah Financial District on Tuesday and wounding 11 people, and the coalition said it had intercepted three ballistic missiles fired from Yemen towards Riyadh the previous Saturday. The Houthis had also claimed a strike on the Ras Tanura refinery, Saudi Arabia's largest, while the coalition said it intercepted four drones bound for the complex.

The Houthis say the Saudi response has been devastating for civilians in Yemen. Houthi media said a Saudi strike killed 11 people in Yemen on Friday, reporting that warplanes had hit a residential building in the al-Turbah area of Taiz province and that most of the dead were children. The Houthi military spokesman, Yahya Saree, said Saudi Arabia had launched 104 air raids and missile strikes across Yemen in 24 hours, hitting the capital, Sanaa, and eight governorates, and claimed they had killed or wounded dozens of civilians, including children. The coalition, for its part, said it had destroyed 50 Houthi military targets and a ballistic missile launcher in Sanaa, Hodeidah and Saada governorates on Friday, but it did not mention the reported strikes on civilian buildings, and the casualty figures put out by either side have not been independently verified.

The exchange is the sharpest single day of fighting between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis since the 2022 truce broke down, and it is unfolding against a wider regional war. The Houthis, who are backed by Iran, have framed their attacks as retaliation for coalition strikes, with Saree saying they were answering raids he said had killed at least 26 people in the Al-Maqatirah district of Lahij province — a figure that has not been independently verified and to which the coalition has not responded. Yemen's Presidential Leadership Council, meanwhile, has issued a law designating the Houthis a terrorist organisation. Saudi air defences have intercepted about 90 per cent of Houthi munitions over the past five months, according to an intelligence assessment reported by The National on Thursday, but the breaches that do get through have wounded 22 people in the capital in three days, and attacks in October on Riyadh's main airport killed at least 14 people and left at least ten international airlines unwilling to fly into the city.

For now, neither side shows any sign of stepping back. The coalition has presented every interception as proof that its defences hold, while the Houthis have kept up a campaign that has moved from the airport to the financial district to the oil coast within a single week, testing the kingdom's interceptor inventory daily for more than 20 consecutive days. The markets, for what they are worth, expect no quick end to the fighting: on Polymarket, traders put the chance of an effective Houthi–Saudi ceasefire by 31 December at just 12 per cent, down 37 per cent over the past month. What happens next may depend on whether the coalition's air campaign degrades the Houthis' ability to keep launching — and on whether the missile war over Riyadh draws in the wider confrontation between the United States and Iran, which is already playing out in the Gulf of Oman.
