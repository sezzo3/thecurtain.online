A Peace Declaration With No Peace in It: Trump, Xi and Putin Leave Shenzhen With Words, Not Deals

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-20 · 5 min read

Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin issued a four-point "peace declaration" on Thursday at the end of the first trilateral summit of the leaders of the United States, China and Russia — a document that reaffirmed that nuclear war "cannot be won and must never be fought", but announced no arms-control talks, mentioned neither the war in Ukraine nor the war with Iran, and committed its signatories to nothing enforceable. The three men met for ninety minutes at the secluded Kylin Villa retreat beside a lake in the wooded hills of Shenzhen, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum, in a gathering Mr Trump later called "bigger than Yalta". It was the first time the leaders of the world's three largest nuclear powers had ever sat down together. It produced a photograph, a statement, and — by the admission of Mr Putin himself — no ambitious goals.

The declaration, published by Russia and reported by China's Xinhua news agency, sets out four points. As permanent members of the United Nations Security Council, the three countries committed to reform the UN so that it can respond more effectively to global challenges. They agreed to increase the production and supply of refined petroleum products for the benefit of all nations, reaffirming the importance of stable energy flows at a time when supplies have been disrupted by the war in Iran. On international law, they said the principle of state consent and respect for the immunity of state officials are of paramount importance, and agreed that the International Criminal Court and similar bodies "must not abuse their jurisdiction" — a pointed passage, since none of the three countries is a member of the court, which has issued an arrest warrant for Mr Putin over alleged war crimes in Ukraine. And on nuclear weapons, the three reaffirmed that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought, and said they would "conduct a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation of nuclear weapons" — without giving any time frame.

What the declaration does not say is as revealing as what it does. It contains no commitment to negotiate a trilateral nuclear arms reduction treaty, the goal Mr Trump has pursued since his first term and which China has consistently rejected, arguing that its arsenal is much smaller than those of the other two powers. New START, the last treaty capping American and Russian strategic arsenals, expired in February, leaving the two largest nuclear powers without any binding limit for the first time in decades. "It's not an easy thing to do — give them up," Mr Trump said of nuclear weapons at the start of the meeting. Daryl Kimball, executive director of the Arms Control Association, called the reaffirmation that nuclear war cannot be won positive, but the statement offered no path to the negotiations Mr Trump has long sought. China's foreign minister, Wang Yi, said only that the countries would maintain coordination at working level after what he called the "peace summit", and Xinhua reported that the leaders agreed to meet regularly, including in China next year.

The two wars that dominate the world's attention were kept out of the text entirely. Mr Putin, fresh from a setback in Ukraine after the crippling of the bridge linking Crimea to Russia, acknowledged in his press conference afterwards that the declaration "did not set ambitious goals" and added that nobody would be forced to comply with its decisions. Mr Trump told NBC afterwards that the meeting "went great" — "On a scale of zero to 10, I think it was a 12. Fantastic relationships built." He greeted Mr Putin as "my friend" and received a hug from Mr Xi, in the latest of the diplomatic spectacles he favours. Mr Xi, quoted by Xinhua, called the meeting "positive, constructive and fruitful", saying the three countries "share a common perspective on safeguarding world peace". The leaders' public remarks as the summit began offered little reassurance to those fearful of a great-power carve-up of the world, and President Emmanuel Macron of France said the declaration would not "lead to a reorganisation of the world".

The summit capped a three-day diplomatic swing through Asia by Mr Trump that produced few tangible victories, despite his repeated boasts about making history and brokering peace. Analysts said he came to Shenzhen seeking to bolster his standing after his Republican Party lost control of Congress in this month's midterm elections. Instead, the trip raised fresh questions among American allies about his increasingly close relationships with Mr Xi and with Mr Putin, who continues to wage war against Ukraine. One analyst, Jonathan Eyal, said Mr Putin had been a winner from the summit, gaining "credibility and escape from pariah status", while Mr Trump "gained a photo opportunity he didn't need". The meeting ended a few hours before Ukrainian officials and the leaders of its main European allies were due to gather in Berlin — a reminder that the country with the most at stake in the war was not in the room, and neither was Europe.

What happens next will be measured in readouts, not handshakes — the same test set before the summit began. If the working-level coordination Mr Wang promised produces a timetable for arms-control talks, or if the separate bilateral tracks — Mr Trump with Mr Xi on trade, Mr Trump with Mr Putin on Ukraine — yield anything verifiable, the day in Shenzhen will have been more than theatre. If they produce nothing, it will confirm the sceptics' view that a summit announced with no agreed agenda and no deal expected was always a photograph in search of a purpose. Mr Trump has now left China. The three leaders have agreed to meet again. Whether they meet again as peacemakers or as the authors of a new great-power order will be decided by what they do, not by what they signed.
