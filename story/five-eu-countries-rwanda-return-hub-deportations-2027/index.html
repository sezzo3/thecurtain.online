Five EU States Plan a Return Hub in Rwanda for Rejected Asylum Seekers

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-17 · 5 min read

Five European Union countries have unveiled plans to send rejected asylum seekers to a deportation centre in Rwanda, in the most concrete step yet by European governments to move migration enforcement outside the bloc. Germany, Denmark, Austria, Greece and the Netherlands announced on Monday that they were immediately starting talks with Rwanda on setting up a "return hub" there under a new European Union framework, with the aim of carrying out the first deportations in 2027. The announcement was made in Vienna by Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker and his Danish counterpart, Mette Frederiksen, with Rwanda's interior minister, Vincent Biruta, present at the press conference.

The hub would hold people who have no legal right to remain in the five countries — those whose asylum claims have been refused and who have exhausted every appeal — while their removal from Europe is arranged. It would not decide asylum claims: unlike Britain's earlier Rwanda scheme, which planned to send asylum seekers to Rwanda to have their cases processed there and was scrapped in 2024 after the United Kingdom's Supreme Court ruled against it, the European plan applies only to people whose claims have already been finally rejected. The first phase is expected to be a pilot of about 100 people, with each of the five countries sending around 20, and the initial phase would focus exclusively on single men, according to BBC reporting. The United Nations' International Organisation for Migration could play a supporting role, and its spokesman said the agency was in discussions with the European countries involved.

The plan follows the approval of the European Union's new Return Regulation, which for the first time allows member states to establish return hubs outside the bloc. The five countries have been working towards a deal for several months, as part of a broader push inside the European Union to increase deportations and curb irregular migration. The politics behind it are plain: centrist governments across Europe are toughening immigration policy to counter growing public support for far-right parties, and the ability to show deportations actually happening has become a test of competence. The European Border and Coast Guard Agency recorded nearly 110,000 irregular border crossings into the EU in the first ten months of 2026, down 29 per cent from the same period a year earlier — a fall the governments can cite as progress, and a number their critics say shows the scale of the problem is already shrinking.

The criticism has been swift and severe. Spain's prime minister, Pedro Sánchez, criticised the proposal, and 45 rights, aid and trade union groups issued a joint statement urging the five countries to abandon the plan, saying neither legal safeguards nor monitoring mechanisms could eliminate the risk of rights abuses. Human Rights Watch said Rwanda failed to meet the human rights standards required under the EU's new Return Regulation, pointing to what it described as a well-established record of repression of dissent, arbitrary detention and ill-treatment in detention, as well as support for armed rebels in the neighbouring Democratic Republic of Congo. The Council of Europe, the pan-European human rights body, warned in July that return hubs posed "considerable human rights risks", and the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights has said any such facility must meet strict conditions. As recently as January, Germany, Greece, Denmark and the Netherlands themselves raised concerns about Rwanda at its United Nations human rights review.

Rwanda, for its part, has been pitching itself to Western governments as a destination for such schemes, and has experience taking in stranded migrants. But its human rights record remains the central objection: critics argue that paying Kigali to host a deportation programme would not only cause suffering to those deported, but would finance and lend political legitimacy to a government they hold responsible for serious abuses at home and abroad. Britain, whose own Rwanda plan collapsed at a cost of about £700 million, has not ruled out joining the arrangement in future, Downing Street said, adding that it would watch how it develops.

What happens next will be decided in negotiations with Kigali and, almost certainly, in court. The five countries have given no details on funding, the size of the facility, or how people held there would access lawyers, contact their families, or challenge their transfer — the gaps that rights groups say make the scheme unlawful under the European Convention on Human Rights. Legal challenges are expected to reach the European courts within months. The pilot, if it goes ahead as described, would move about 100 people; the question it will answer is whether Europe has found a workable way to enforce its asylum decisions, or whether it has simply exported a problem it could not solve at home.
