Hundreds of Thousands Rally in Prague Against Plan to Put Public Media on the State Budget

Sezgin Baktiaya · 2026-11-16 · 5 min read

Hundreds of thousands of Czechs filled Prague's Letná Plain on Sunday in what organisers said may have been the largest demonstration in the country since the Velvet Revolution of 1989, protesting against a government plan to scrap the licence fee that funds public television and radio and pay for both broadcasters directly from the state budget. The rally, organised by the civic group Milion chvilek (A Million Moments for Democracy) under the banner "Letná Against 4:36", drew an estimated 350,000 to 450,000 people, according to the organisers, with the group's chairman, Mikuláš Minář, putting the figure at about 400,000. That would exceed the anti-government protest of more than 200,000 people at the same site in March, and the organisers predicted it could end up as the largest civic gathering since the demonstrations that swept away Communist rule and propelled the dissident playwright Václav Havel to power. Prague police said they do not publish crowd estimates, and by early evening they reported no disturbances. Protesters clogged nearby metro stations and filled the park overlooking the capital, carrying signs that read "Hands off the media" and "Let media deal with politicians".

At issue is a draft bill that would cancel the 205-crown (about $9.26) monthly household broadcasting fee — 150 crowns for television and 55 for radio — that currently funds Czech Television and Czech Radio, and replace it with money from the state budget. Critics say the change would dent the broadcasters' independence and viability: a 15 per cent financing cut proposed as part of the overhaul would reduce television's nominal budget to the level of 2008 and radio's to that of 2005. The bill passed a preliminary vote in the lower house of parliament in the early hours of 24 October — at 4:36 a.m., the hour that gave the protest its name — and awaits further debate after the parliamentary committee considering it suspended its deliberations on 30 October. The proposal was introduced in April by Culture Minister Oto Klempíř. Its supporters in government say the fee is outdated; its opponents say funding from the state budget would put the broadcasters' budgets at the mercy of whichever government is in power.

The government behind the bill is that of the populist billionaire Prime Minister Andrej Babiš, backed by the far-right SPD and the eurosceptic Motorists parties, which accuses Czech private and public media of bias. Opposition parties have compared the proposals to the changes to public media in Hungary under former Prime Minister Viktor Orbán, and the European Commission, along with political groups in the European Parliament, has expressed concern about threats to the independence of the public broadcasters. The European Parliament held a plenary debate on media freedom and the rule of law in the Czech Republic on 11 November. Mr Minář, addressing the crowd, framed the protest as a defence of democratic limits on power. "An elected government has the right to govern but it does not have the right to do whatever it wants and to dismantle democratic institutions," he said.

The rally was deliberately staged as an echo of 1989. It took place two days before Tuesday's public holiday commemorating the start of the Velvet Revolution, on the same Letná Plain that hosted mass rallies during the revolution itself, and many protesters wore pointed hats folded out of newspapers — a tribute to the animated character who has introduced Czech Television's nightly children's bedtime stories for decades. Speakers included students, soldiers and former dissidents who signed the Charter 77 human rights initiative. The demonstration was broadcast live by the public news television channel, the ČTK news agency and a few independent media outlets. The protest followed a one-day strike by Czech public television and radio workers on 3 November, which drew support from labour unions, universities, students, religious organisations, artists, athletes and international media organisations.

The reaction online matched the scale on the ground. A thread on r/europe reporting that around 400,000 people had gathered in Prague to defend public media drew more than 14,000 upvotes and 229 comments, with commenters praising the defence of independent media and debating the crowd figures and the government's direction. The turnout figures themselves remain contested — the organisers' estimate is the only number available, since the police do not publish one — but even the lower end of the organisers' range would make Sunday's rally one of the largest gatherings in Czech history, and the largest since the country became independent in 1993. For a government that won power promising to take on what it calls a biased media establishment, the sight of Letná full two days before the Velvet Revolution anniversary is a measure of how far that fight has escalated.

What happens next will be decided in parliament and in public. The bill still has to complete its passage through the lower house, and Mr Minář has invited government representatives to a public discussion at Prague's Švanda Theatre at 7 p.m. on 2 December, according to the Czech news agency ČTK. Tuesday's anniversary commemorations will keep the symbolism of 1989 in front of the public, and the government will have to decide whether to press ahead with a plan that has now drawn the biggest street opposition of its term, or to offer the safeguards for broadcaster independence that the protesters and the European Parliament have demanded. For the Czech public broadcasters, whose staff have already struck once over the plan, the question is whether a fee paid by households can survive a government that would rather pay for them itself — and whether the crowd on Letná has changed the arithmetic in parliament.
