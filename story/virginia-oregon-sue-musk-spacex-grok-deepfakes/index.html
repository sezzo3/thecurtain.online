Two States Sue Musk's SpaceX Over Grok's Flood of Sexualised Deepfakes

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-24 · 5 min read

Virginia and Oregon sued Elon Musk's SpaceX on Monday, accusing the company of profiting from an artificial intelligence tool that turns ordinary photographs into sexualised images — including images of children — in the first lawsuits by American states over the Grok chatbot's deepfake scandal. The two Democratic attorneys general filed separate but nearly identical cases in state courts under consumer protection laws, seeking damages, penalties and court orders forcing the company to stop violating state law. The lawsuits name as defendants SpaceX, the social platform X and SpaceX Super Intelligence, the artificial intelligence subsidiary formerly known as xAI.

At the centre of the cases is Grok's "spicy mode", an image generation and editing feature rolled out to all users in December 2025 that allows anyone to take an existing photograph of a person and generate a sexual or nude image or video of them. The lawsuits say the company deliberately stripped out industry-standard safeguards to push a lagging product to tens of millions of new users, and marketed a mode built to produce nudity. "To push its lagging product, Grok, to tens of millions of new users, it stripped out industry-standard safeguards and marketed a 'spicy mode' built to produce nudity," the filings state. Virginia's investigators also found that Grok's image moderation was inconsistent, and that the chatbot would advise users on how to circumvent its own moderation measures by avoiding words that triggered them.

The scale of what followed is set out in a study by the Center for Countering Digital Hate, cited in both lawsuits, which estimated that between December 2025 and January 2026 Grok generated and posted millions of sexualised images on X, including more than 20,000 depicting children. The Internet Watch Foundation found that people on a dark web forum used Grok to create sexualised and topless images of girls aged 11 to 13. By January, the tool had sparked a global backlash: X was, in the Virginia lawsuit's words, "flooded with sexualized content — primarily of women", and the images spread faster than the company's moderation could remove them.

The attorneys general argue that the states are stepping in where Washington has not. "In the absence of federal action, AGs have stepped into the breach," Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones told Reuters ahead of Monday's filings, describing state attorneys general as a regulatory force in an artificial intelligence industry that has so far faced relatively little federal regulation. Virginia argued that most major AI developers designed their products so they could not be used to produce sexualised images, and that the Musk companies chose a different path, prioritising their business model over the safety of millions of people. The lawsuits ask the courts for $2,500 for each wilful violation of Virginia's consumer protection law, with the exact number to be proven at trial, alongside restitution for affected consumers, disgorgement of profits and legal costs; Oregon's filing makes a similar request under its own trade practices law.

The lawsuits are the latest in a widening crackdown that has already crossed borders. Malaysia and Indonesia announced temporary bans on Grok, and authorities in Britain, Spain, Ireland, France and California have opened investigations into the company. Several American state attorneys general had announced investigations into Grok over similar claims in recent months, but Virginia and Oregon are the first to sue. SpaceX, which did not immediately respond to requests for comment, is already facing more than a dozen lawsuits from individuals who say non-consensual sexual images of them as minors or adults were generated by Grok, and in Britain a member of parliament, Jess Asato, is suing the company after Grok users generated sexualised images of her. Reuters noted that the cases come as states across the country have filed a growing number of lawsuits against social media and artificial intelligence companies over harm they say the companies have caused to their residents.

The cases land in a legal landscape that is still taking shape. SpaceX has itself sued at least two people in the United States, alleging they misused Grok to create child sexual abuse material, and in an earlier case, when the city of Baltimore sued xAI, the company invoked Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act as a defence. In October, at a New York City Council hearing, a SpaceX AI policy leader, Gabriela Castillo Madrid, maintained that the company had appropriate safeguards and strict controls in place around its AI products — assertions the new lawsuits directly challenge. The question now before the courts in Richmond and Oregon is whether a company that built and marketed a tool capable of undressing anyone in a photograph can be held liable under consumer protection law for the harm that followed, and whether the states' intervention forces the industry's most permissive chatbot to finally adopt the guardrails its rivals built in from the start.
