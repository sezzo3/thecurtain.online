Russian Jets Trigger a Shelter Alert on Ulleung Island — Then Seoul's Own Messages Contradict Each Other

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-24 · 5 min read

Several Russian military aircraft entered South Korea's air defence identification zone near Ulleung Island on Tuesday morning, prompting a brief air-raid warning that sent residents of the remote island scrambling for shelter, before the alert was lifted within minutes. The Joint Chiefs of Staff said the aircraft approached Ulleung Island in the East Sea, the body of water also known as the Sea of Japan, but did not violate South Korean airspace. The military had detected the planes before they entered the zone and scrambled Air Force fighter jets, including F-15Ks, to take tactical measures while the Russian aircraft withdrew. No major disruptions were reported on the island, home to about 9,000 people.

For the residents, the morning was confusing as well as frightening. At 7:52 a.m., the Ministry of the Interior and Safety sent an emergency text message instructing Ulleung County residents to take shelter in underground facilities or inside buildings, saying Russian aircraft had violated Korean airspace. Three minutes later the ministry sent another message saying the aircraft had left the danger zone and the warning was lifted. Then, at 7:58 a.m., the North Gyeongsang provincial government sent a different message saying the aircraft believed to be North Korean had left the danger zone. It took about forty minutes for a correction to arrive, confirming that the aircraft were Russian after all.

The provincial government later explained the error: officials had reused the wording of a standard training template that referred to a presumed North Korean aircraft, changing only the time before sending it out. The episode laid bare how quickly an already tense situation can be made worse by a copy-and-paste mistake. The military's own account was clear from the start: the aircraft were Russian, they had entered the air defence identification zone, known as the Kadiz, and they had not crossed into sovereign South Korean airspace. An air defence identification zone is a buffer extending beyond a country's territorial airspace, giving its military more time to identify, track and respond to approaching aircraft, and entering it is not itself a violation of airspace.

The incident was not a one-off. Tuesday's flight was the third time this month that Russian military aircraft have entered the zone over the East Sea, following similar entries on 17 and 19 November, and the Joint Chiefs said Russian aircraft had breached the zone several times this year. The military did not say how many planes it detected on Tuesday or what types they were, and said it planned to lodge a protest with Moscow through the Russian military attaché. The pattern fits a wider season of Russian probing around the region, with Moscow also issuing warnings to Finland and Estonia in recent days as tensions with NATO remain high.

For the people of Ulleung, a volcanic island some 120 kilometres off South Korea's east coast, the alert was a reminder of how exposed their home is. Residents checked emergency text messages on their phones as civil defence sirens sounded, and the confusion over whether the aircraft were Russian or North Korean — two very different threats in the South Korean imagination — left many unsure what they had just been warned about. The island's rugged cliffs and small fishing communities are a long way from the politics of the peninsula, but the air defence zone that surrounds them is one of the most closely watched stretches of sky in Asia.

Seoul will now watch for Moscow's response to the planned protest, and for whether the flights continue. The episode is likely to feed a debate in South Korea about the reliability of its emergency alert system, which issued a warning, lifted it, and then contradicted itself, all within the space of an hour. It also underlines a harder truth for the region's militaries: in a crowded sky, where Russian, Chinese and North Korean aircraft all operate close to allied airspace, the difference between a routine interception and a crisis can come down to a single misunderstood message.
