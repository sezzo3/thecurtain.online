Iran Hits US Air Base in Kuwait and Renews Attacks on Bahrain in Retaliation for American Strikes

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

Iran widened its attacks on its Gulf neighbours early on Wednesday, firing drones at the United States-hosted Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait and launching several fresh attacks on Bahrain, in what appeared to be retaliation for American strikes the day before. Kuwait said its air defences intercepted the drones and reported no damage, while Bahrain said its forces had intercepted a number of Iranian attacks and issued shelter warnings before giving the all-clear. It was the first Iranian attack on Kuwait in more than two months, and the latest in a rapid sequence of strikes that has put the Gulf's American military installations back at the centre of a war now in its ninth month.

According to a report on Iran's state broadcaster, the Iranian military fired combat and kamikaze drones at air defence systems and drone and missile launch platforms at Ali Al Salem, a Kuwaiti air base about 37 kilometres from the Iraqi border that hosts American forces. Iran claimed the operation had destroyed Patriot batteries and launch positions used by the United States military at the base. That claim could not be independently confirmed, and Kuwait's military did not identify the origin of the drones, say how many were involved, or report any damage or casualties. Kuwait's armed forces said its air defences were responding to hostile drone threats and told residents that explosions they could hear were caused by interception operations, while the Foreign Ministry condemned the repeated Iranian attacks as "criminal", saying they threatened the country's security and stability. The United States military did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Bahrain, which hosts the US Navy's 5th Fleet, came under renewed attack in the same hours. The Interior Ministry issued two alerts early on Wednesday urging people to take shelter, followed by the all-clear a short time later, and the Bahrain Defence Force said its air defences had repelled several Iranian aerial attacks before they could reach their objectives, accusing Tehran of a "systematic hostile stance" towards the kingdom. The Foreign Ministry said the threats had been neutralised before reaching their targets. No information was given on any damage or casualties. It was the third Iranian attack on Bahrain in five days: the Revolutionary Guards had already claimed strikes on American facilities there on Friday and Sunday, and on Tuesday they said their drones had targeted a US-operated radar system at Sheikh Isa Air Base, an attack Bahrain and the United States said had been intercepted, with US Central Command reporting no injuries among American personnel and no damage to American equipment.

The exchanges follow a direct tit-for-tat sequence. On Tuesday, a US fighter jet struck a Revolutionary Guard missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, in retaliation for the Iranian attack on the radar installation in Bahrain earlier that day. Iran said its recent attacks were in response to US drone surveillance operations and an attack on Iranian communications towers. The pattern resumed on Friday 13 November, the first Iranian attack on a Gulf state since early September, after months in which Tehran had largely held back from striking its neighbours. The war itself began on 28 February, when Israel and the United States attacked Iran, and Iran has regularly fired on American facilities and interests in neighbouring Gulf countries, as well as energy infrastructure, ever since.

The stakes extend well beyond the two bases. With its economy suffering under a US naval blockade and American military escorts helping to restore the passage of oil tankers through the Strait of Hormuz, experts say Tehran may be growing weary of the status quo — and determined to raise the price of maintaining it. Iran has continued to attack tankers as they attempt to transit the strait: the United Arab Emirates state-owned oil company ADNOC said on Wednesday that one of its tanker crew members was missing after an attack in the strait on Tuesday, while 11 others were injured and were receiving medical care. Ali Al Salem itself has been struck at least 30 times by Iran during the war, damaging or destroying at least 15 aircraft hangars, numerous warehouses and 10 buildings near a residential area, according to an Associated Press report. The same day, Saudi air defences intercepted and destroyed two cruise missiles fired towards Riyadh by Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen, a day after coalition forces launched a major air attack across rebel-held areas of Yemen that the Houthis said killed at least 17 people — and days after Saudi-backed forces withdrew from areas around the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, consolidating the Houthis' grip on the waterway.

What happens next is genuinely uncertain, and analysts on both sides of the argument agree on the danger. "Even if neither side intends to enter a broader confrontation, the accumulation of military incidents across multiple fronts increases the risk of miscalculation and escalation beyond what either side originally intended," Hamidreza Azizi, a senior Iran analyst at the International Crisis Group, wrote after the first reports of the attack on Kuwait. Danny Citrinowicz, a researcher at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies, described an "increasingly clear" pattern of "deliberate, calibrated escalation", arguing that Iran's objective is not necessarily to trigger an all-out war but to steadily increase the military, economic and political costs of maintaining the blockade — a dilemma for Washington, where a limited response may encourage Iran to push further while a stronger one risks accelerating the escalation the United States is trying to avoid. President Donald Trump has sent mixed messages, saying new strikes are highly unlikely while also sending a third aircraft carrier and thousands more troops to the Middle East, and negotiations for a diplomatic solution have stagnated despite intensified mediation efforts from Qatar. As Citrinowicz put it, there are two clear paths to de-escalation: a negotiated agreement, or an American decision to lift the blockade.
