Milburn Demands a 'Complete System Reset' to Rescue a Million Young People from Joblessness

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-17 · 5 min read

Britain should tear up the way it supports its young people and start again, according to a major government-commissioned review published in full on Tuesday, which warns that almost a million 16- to 24-year-olds are now outside education, employment or training. Alan Milburn, the former Labour health secretary who led the review, calls for a "complete system reset" and a ten-year plan to cut the share of young people known as Neets from 13 per cent — the highest since 2014 — to around 5 per cent by 2036, bringing about 440,000 more young people into work. "Britain has had a youth Neet problem for 25 years and never a plan to end it," he said. "We are at a fork in the road and half measures will not do."

At the heart of the 278-page report, titled "From a Welfare State to a Working State: A new settlement for the next generation", is a new Youth Opportunity Service in England, giving every young person a legal right to support. Each young person would get a named adviser and a personal plan, with a guaranteed six-month paid job after a year of engagement, and advisers tasked with securing work, an apprenticeship, a college place or supported employment within four months. The review also reaches back to the start of life: it wants 90 per cent of children arriving at school ready to learn by the age of five, up from 68 per cent in England today, and a doubling of apprenticeships over the decade, alongside faster mental-health help that does not require a diagnosis.

The plan is paid for, in part, by the most contested part of the package: a rebuilt benefits system for under-25s. Young people aged 18 to 24 would be barred from claiming the two main sickness and disability benefits — the health element of universal credit and personal independence payments — and moved onto a new youth allowance, paid at the standard universal credit rate of £338.58 a month, with a separate additional-costs allowance for those currently receiving Pip. The review says the system now spends £25 on youth benefits for every £1 spent helping young people into work or education; by 2036 it wants that ratio at £2 to £1. The extra employment support would cost close to £6 billion a year by 2036–37, with £500 million in the first year, and Milburn accepts the books would not balance for a decade.

The warning behind the numbers is stark. Without reform, the review's modelling says the number of Neets would rise by more than a quarter to 1.27 million by 2031 — one in six young people — and youth disengagement already costs the country about £125 billion a year. "A lost generation is not inevitable," Milburn said. "A working generation is possible." He also urged caution on the government's flagship employment-rights legislation, warning that moving too fast on the crackdown on zero-hours contracts could deter employers from hiring young people, and suggested deferring the ambition to bring the youth minimum wage into line with the rate for older workers.

The politics are already moving. Andy Burnham has made clear he will accept the review's core findings, saying the status quo cannot continue, and legislation to implement the reforms is expected early next year, with a white paper answering the hardest questions — who qualifies for the new benefits, and at what rates — to follow. But the benefits overhaul has drawn fire from disability charities and some Labour MPs, who warn it could leave young disabled people worse off, and from the Conservative shadow work and pensions secretary, Helen Whately, who criticised parts of the plan as overly reliant on government intervention. A new "youth opportunity test" would require major tax, spending and regulatory decisions to be assessed on their effect on the next generation before they are taken.

What happens next will be decided in the white paper and in Parliament, where the eligibility rules for the new allowances — the questions Milburn's review leaves open — will be fought over by the work and pensions secretary, Pat McFadden, and his critics. The review's backers say the test is simple: whether a government can find £6 billion a year for employment support now, in the hope of savings a decade away, and whether a guarantee of work for every young person can survive contact with a labour market that has failed them for 25 years. If it can, Britain would go from one of Europe's worst performers on youth participation to one of its best. If it cannot, Milburn's warning stands: a lost generation is a choice, and Britain is still making it.
