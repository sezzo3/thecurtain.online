Spain Votes on Sunday With Sánchez Defiant as Polls Point to a Right-Wing Majority

Sezgin Baktiaya · 2026-11-28 · 5 min read

Spain's party leaders have held their final rallies before Sunday's general election, with Socialist Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez insisting he can still defy the polls and his conservative rival Alberto Núñez Feijóo promising a new political era, as the last published surveys point to a majority for the right and the far right.

Mr Sánchez closed his campaign in Madrid on Friday, taking to the stage with his wife, Begoña Gómez, in a red T-shirt and telling supporters: "I cannot imagine us losing." He accused his opponents of hurling insults and lies, and warned that a government formed by Mr Feijóo's People's Party with the support of the far-right Vox party would reverse years of social progress and gut public services. "The elections are about moving Spain forward, not moving it backwards," he said, urging left-wing voters to turn out in large numbers and arguing that a progressive majority remained possible. The Oscar-winning film director Pedro Almodóvar was among those at the rally. Mr Sánchez has campaigned on having boosted the Spanish economy while its European partners struggled, and has become a standard-bearer for Europeans frustrated with the Trump administration.

Mr Feijóo, whose People's Party is narrowly ahead in the opinion polls, spent the final hours of the campaign travelling first to Barcelona and then to Seville, the capital of Andalusia, the region with the most seats in Spain's 350-seat parliament. "This time, yes, we're going to do it," he told supporters, in a nod to his failure to form a government three years ago despite winning the most seats. He dismissed Mr Sánchez's campaign as one of "fear", telling supporters in Seville: "When a government has nothing to offer but fear, it means it has nothing else left to offer." Mr Feijóo, who built his career running central public bodies and the north-western region of Galicia, said he wanted to maintain good relations with President Donald Trump, advocated tough immigration policies and stronger border management, and promised his party would build a million homes over four years. His government, if he forms one, would depend on Vox, led by Santiago Abascal, whose final rally filled a bullring in Valencia with Spanish flags and green Vox banners. Mr Abascal told supporters that Mr Sánchez's government had "betrayed" Spaniards, and his party, which favours mass deportations, hopes to almost double the 33 seats it won three years ago.

The final published polls, released before a legal blackout on Monday, point to a close but clear outcome. The People's Party is forecast at about 31 per cent of the vote and 128 seats, against 28.7 per cent and 116 seats for the Socialists and 18.2 per cent and 61 seats for Vox, putting the right and far right at 187 to 189 of the 350 seats in Congress, where a majority requires 176. The final survey by 40dB, based on 7,500 interviews, put the People's Party at 31.8 per cent, the Socialists at 30.9 per cent and Vox at 17.3 per cent, and simulations suggested the People's Party and Vox would reach an absolute majority in about 90 per cent of runs even with polling errors a quarter larger than in past elections. Almost 39 million people have a vote, and commentators expect a high turnout: Mr Sánchez has argued that turnout near 75 per cent would help the left, and one expert, Paloma Román of Madrid's Complutense University, estimated it could reach around 78 per cent.

Housing dominated the final days of the campaign, after a four-way television debate on Thursday exposed Spain's sharply polarised politics. Mr Feijóo promised a million new homes over four years, while Mr Sánchez said housing should be a right, not an object of speculation and profit. The conservatives tried to turn the spotlight on the series of scandals that have overshadowed the Socialists' term in office, while Mr Sánchez and his left-wing ally Ada Colau, who heads the Frente Amplio, focused on women's rights. Mr Feijóo's decision to end his campaign in Seville was significant: Andalusia has 61 seats, more than any other region, and is run by a coalition of the People's Party and Vox, as are Extremadura, Aragón and Castilla y León. On Reddit, a thread in r/spain warned that migrants had fuelled Spain's boom and that investors feared an anti-immigration government could stifle the economy, a measure of the anxieties the campaign has stirred.

The vote will be watched far beyond Spain. Mr Sánchez leads one of Europe's few remaining left-wing governments, and with France's presidential election five months away, there is a real chance that two of the continent's biggest economies could move to the right in quick succession. Sunday's election also opens a round of votes across the continent over the next twelve months that could reshape the balance of power inside the European Union. The betting markets have already made up their minds: on Polymarket, traders put the chance of a People's Party–Vox majority at 86 per cent, and give the People's Party a 74 per cent chance of winning the most seats. Whether Mr Sánchez can produce another of the comebacks for which he is known — he is widely seen as the great survivor of Spanish politics — will be decided when almost 39 million Spaniards vote on Sunday.
