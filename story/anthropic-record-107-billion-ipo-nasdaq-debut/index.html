The $107 Billion Debut: Anthropic's Record Listing Lights Up a Falling Market

Sezgin Baktiaya · 2026-11-20 · 5 min read

Anthropic, the maker of the Claude chatbot, became the first frontier artificial intelligence laboratory to list on a public stock exchange on Thursday, closing 17 per cent above its offer price in the largest initial public offering on record — and doing so on a day when almost everything else on Wall Street fell. The company priced its offering at $83 a share on Wednesday, above the marketed range of $70 to $78, and sold 980 million Class A shares to raise $81.3 billion, beating the record set by SpaceX's listing in June. A simultaneous $26 billion private placement involving Nvidia, Microsoft, Google and others brought total proceeds to about $107.3 billion. Shares opened on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT at $111.50, a 34 per cent premium to the offer price, touched an intraday high near $115, and closed at $97.17 — briefly pushing the company's fully diluted market value past $2 trillion before it settled at about $1.8 trillion, making Anthropic the world's seventh-biggest public company on its first day of trading.

The numbers behind the debut were extraordinary even by the standards of a year that has already broken the record for American listings. Book-building drew about $500 billion of demand — more than seven times the shares on offer — with institutional orders of roughly $400 billion and about $100 billion from retail investors. More than half of the institutional investors who bid ultimately received no shares at all, while retail investors took about 15 per cent of the offering and generated roughly $1.4 billion of turnover on the day, a record for a listing.

The offering was led by Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, with twenty other banks participating. Data compiled by Bloomberg showed that including Anthropic, the total raised in American IPOs in 2026 approached $246 billion, surpassing the record of $195 billion set in 2021. The listing also turned thousands of employees into millionaires: Anthropic disclosed that it had granted about 5,300 employees 1.77 billion stock options at $2.91 a share and more than a billion restricted stock units, worth about $232 billion at the offer price — an average of $44 million per employee — while the combined wealth of its seven founders reached $166 billion.

Anthropic's business, by the figures in its filings, is growing at a pace that helps explain the frenzy. The company, founded in 2021 by the chief executive Dario Amodei, a former OpenAI researcher, and his sister Daniela Amodei, disclosed an annualised revenue run rate of $81 billion as of the end of October, up $10 billion from the end of September, with third-quarter revenue of about $17 billion. Its listing ceremony at the Nasdaq MarketSite in Times Square was attended by Mr Amodei, his co-founders and Paul Smith, the company's chief commercial officer, who was appointed its first chief operating officer in July last year. The debut puts Anthropic well ahead of its great rival OpenAI, which does not expect to list before 2027, and gives the public markets their first direct benchmark for a frontier AI laboratory — a company whose product is the technology itself, rather than a device or a service built on top of it.

But the listing landed in a hostile market, and its success did nothing to lift it. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 2.19 per cent, closing below 50,000 for the first time since May; the S&P 500 lost 2.02 per cent; the Nasdaq Composite dropped 2.25 per cent; and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index declined 2.42 per cent. It was the fifth straight session of losses for American stocks, and the selling spread to Asia on Friday, with Japan's Nikkei down 4.8 per cent and SoftBank falling more than 16 per cent. Anthropic's own shares gave back part of their gain in after-hours trading, falling about 5 per cent. Investors are grappling with worries over Nvidia's role as a major backstop of the AI ecosystem following its longtime chief's ouster, over the scale of AI infrastructure spending, and over a Democratic sweep in the midterm elections that promises to change the industry's regulatory landscape. California's attorney general served an investigative subpoena on Nvidia this week, seeking to determine whether advanced chips reached China.

The contradiction at the heart of the day — a record-breaking AI listing in the middle of an AI sell-off — is the question the market will spend the coming months answering. Mohamed El-Erian, the economist, described the debut as the market "making room" for AI: investors still want the exposure, even as they sell the rest of the sector. The optimists say Anthropic's $81 billion revenue run rate and its $107 billion war chest prove the AI boom has a real business underneath it, and that the money raised will fund the infrastructure and applications that come next. The sceptics say a $1.8 trillion valuation for a five-year-old company, priced at more than twenty times its annualised revenue in a falling market, is the definition of a bubble — and point to the after-hours slide as the first sign of doubt. Either way, the listing has changed the structure of the market: the most important AI company in the world is now a public one, its numbers are public, and every quarter from now on will be judged in the open.
