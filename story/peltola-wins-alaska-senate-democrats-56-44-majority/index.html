Peltola Takes Alaska and Completes a 56-Seat Democratic Senate Majority

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

Democrat Mary Peltola has won Alaska's Senate race, defeating two-term Republican Dan Sullivan and completing the Democrats' sweep of the 2026 midterms. The Associated Press called the race on Tuesday evening, two weeks after Election Day, when updated results put Ms Peltola at 50.1 per cent of the vote against 45.8 per cent for Mr Sullivan, with 99 per cent of ballots counted. By clearing half the vote she avoided the ranked-choice tabulation Alaska had scheduled for Wednesday, and her victory gives the Democrats a 56-44 majority in the Senate when the new Congress takes its seats in January.

The result is historic on several counts. Ms Peltola, who is Yup'ik, becomes the first Alaska Native ever to serve in the United States Senate, and the first Democrat to win a Senate race in Alaska since Mark Begich in 2008. She is only the fifth Democrat to represent the state in the Senate since statehood. Her win is the ninth seat the Democrats flipped from Republican control in this year's midterms, a haul that turned what was expected to be a narrow battle for the chamber into a commanding majority. Alaska also elected a Democrat as governor for the first time since 1998, with former state lawmaker Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins defeating three Republicans, including one endorsed by President Donald Trump.

The campaign was one of the most expensive and bitterly fought of the cycle, with more than $140 million spent by both sides, according to the Wall Street Journal. Mr Trump and Vice President JD Vance both visited Alaska in the final weeks to campaign for Mr Sullivan, who pitched himself as the more effective lawmaker and sought to tie Ms Peltola to national Democratic figures, including former President Joe Biden, railing against "lower 48 liberals" he said did not understand Alaska's way of life. Ms Peltola, running on the slogan "fish, family and freedom", deliberately distanced herself from her party at the national level, presenting herself as more supportive of border security, gun rights and the oil-and-gas industry, and ran advertisements pushing back against Republican attacks on her position on transgender rights.

The race was upended by a candidate with the same name as the incumbent. A retired teacher, Dan J. Sullivan — dubbed "Decoy Dan" — qualified for the ballot and advanced to the general election, winning 1.7 per cent of the vote, while another Republican, Gerald Heikes, took 2.1 per cent. Republicans accused Democrats of recruiting the second Mr Sullivan to confuse voters, a charge the party denied, but the New York Times reported that Democratic operatives working on behalf of Ms Peltola had recruited him. Alaska's Republican governor, Mike Dunleavy, launched an investigation into the efforts described in the Times report, and Mr Trump called for Ms Peltola to be prosecuted and removed from the ballot. Days before the election, the Trump administration took the remarkable step of filing a lawsuit seeking to block certification of the results if Mr Sullivan lost narrowly; a federal judge dismissed the suit in early November, though the Justice Department has filed a notice to appeal, and Mr Dunleavy has raised the possibility that the state could withhold certification while its investigation is under way.

Ms Peltola, 53, held Alaska's at-large House seat from 2022 to 2025, serving as the state's first Native American elected to Congress, and lost re-election in 2024 to Republican Nick Begich, though she significantly outperformed her party's showing in the presidential election that year. She was recruited for the Senate run by the Senate minority leader, Chuck Schumer, as a central part of the Democrats' plan to retake the chamber in a state Mr Trump won by 13 percentage points in 2024. Her campaign was shaken in September by a New York Times report alleging staff mistreatment, including that she had shoved an aide in one outburst; her campaign denied a pattern of abusing her staff but said she "sometimes struggled to deal with her grief" after the deaths of her mother and husband in 2023. She declared victory on Saturday, while the count was still under way, and on Tuesday said her election sent the message that the "status quo in DC is not working for Alaska".

For the Democrats, the scale of the result is the story. Mr Schumer celebrated the victory in a statement, saying a 56-seat majority was "an unimaginable result even just a few months ago" and that Democrats were now "in a prime position to create a durable majority that can create lasting, real results for the American people". The online reaction in Alaska itself was jubilant and partisan in equal measure: a post titled "Senator Mary Peltola" on r/alaska drew more than 1,200 upvotes and 162 comments as the count moved her way, and prediction-market traders on Polymarket had priced her victory at 95 per cent or better before the call. Ms Peltola will be sworn in with the new Congress on 3 January, alongside Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota, with whom she will share the distinction of being one of the first female Native American senators.

What happens next will test whether the majority is durable or merely large. The Democrats will arrive in January with room to lose votes and still pass legislation, a cushion that changes the arithmetic for Mr Trump's agenda in the second half of his term. But the Alaska result itself is not yet fully closed: Mr Dunleavy's investigation continues, the Justice Department's appeal is pending, and the question of certification still hangs over a race decided by fewer than 13,000 votes. For now, though, the last Senate contest of the 2026 midterms is settled, and it settled in the Democrats' favour by the widest margin anyone in Washington had dared to predict.
