A Rapper, a Gang and a Diplomat's Expulsion: Denmark Accuses Russia of a Murder Plot on Its Soil

The Curtain Editorial · 2026-11-22 · 5 min read

Denmark accused Russia on Friday of ordering the attempted assassination of a Ukrainian-American rapper on Danish soil — and expelled Moscow's defence attaché in response, in the most serious allegation of Russian state violence inside a NATO country since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine began. The Danish security and intelligence service, PET, said Russian intelligence services planned the killing of the rapper known as Kyivstoner, whose real name is Albert Vasiliev, and that police foiled the plot on 25 September, stopping three men in a car near a music venue in Hellerup, north of Copenhagen, minutes before he was due to perform. Inside the vehicle, officers found firearms and knives. Two men were charged on Friday with attempted murder and assisting a foreign intelligence service, and appeared in court the same day. "We know the Russian intelligence services are behind this. We are absolutely certain," said the Danish foreign minister, Lars Løkke Rasmussen.

The plot, according to PET, was orchestrated with the help of Loyal to Familia, a banned Danish street gang known for extensive, serious and violent criminal activity. Rasmus Kieffer-Kristensen, the head of PET, told journalists: "It is our clear assessment that the assassination attempt was planned by the Russian intelligence services." He added: "A Russian assassination attempt is not something we've seen in Denmark before, and that gives me an impression of an increasingly aggressive and risk-taking Russia." The target is a 34-year-old rapper, comedian and streamer of modest fame — his most popular songs have gathered 1.2 million streams on Spotify and 31 million views on YouTube — who holds both Ukrainian and American citizenship. Russia's FSB had already accused him in July of plotting a drone attack near Moscow, an accusation he denied, and Danish officials said this was the second time Russia had allegedly targeted him.

The diplomatic response was swift. Mr Rasmussen said Denmark had summoned the Russian ambassador and notified him of the government's decision to expel the defence attaché from Copenhagen within seven days. The prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, said Denmark would issue fewer visas to Russians and would seek tougher sanctions against Russia, including a halt to all non-essential visas for Russian citizens, with any visas granted valid only in the issuing country. The Russian embassy in Copenhagen called the Danish allegations baseless, saying the notion that Russia would assassinate "a musician with limited political influence and questionable fame" was absurd, and complained that Denmark had presented no evidence and submitted no request for legal assistance. The European Commission president, Ursula von der Leyen, reproached Russia for the alleged scheme: "I strongly condemn the assassination attempt against a US-Ukrainian citizen in Denmark, orchestrated by Russian intelligence services. This was an attack on EU soil. And it is unacceptable. Russia's hybrid threats are becoming less and less hybrid, and more and more real."

Denmark is now trying to turn a national incident into a European response. On Friday evening it sent a message to EU governments, seen by Reuters and confirmed by two diplomats, urging member states to take "concrete actions" demonstrating European strength against Russia after the foiled plot. The message said the bloc should impose further restrictions on Russian nationals' access to the Schengen zone, including a halt to tourist visas for Russian citizens and a Schengen-wide policy of non-recognition of Russian non-biometric passports. Denmark will present its proposals at a meeting of EU ambassadors in Brussels on Monday. The push comes against a wider pattern of alleged Russian activity in Denmark: in September, PET said Russia was recruiting Danish citizens to help plan sabotage against Danish defence firms with ties to Ukraine.

The case fits into a longer and darker European story. Across the continent, governments have spent the past four years cataloguing what they call Russia's hybrid war — sabotage, arson, cyberattacks, disinformation and the weaponisation of migration — and the line between covert pressure and open violence has been blurring. An assassination attempt carried out through a local criminal gang, against a dual US-Ukrainian citizen, in a quiet suburb of Copenhagen, would be a sharp escalation even by that standard: not a spy swapped in the shadows, but a killing ordered, in Denmark's assessment, by a foreign intelligence service and outsourced to organised crime. Moscow's denial follows the pattern of every previous allegation — no evidence, no cooperation, no admission — and leaves European governments to decide, once again, how to respond to an act they cannot prove in court but are certain happened.

What happens next will be decided in Brussels on Monday, when Denmark puts its proposals for EU-wide travel curbs on the table — and by whether other member states, many of which have their own catalogues of Russian plots, are willing to turn a Danish rapper's narrow escape into a collective punishment of Russian travellers. For Kyivstoner, the immediate question is simpler: whether he can ever perform in public again without looking over his shoulder. Online, the story travelled fast: on Reddit, a r/europe thread on Denmark's call for EU-wide travel restrictions on Russians drew 3,888 upvotes and 174 comments, while a r/worldnews thread on barring Russian tourists drew 4,495 upvotes. The anger in those threads was directed less at the rapper than at the precedent — that a European capital could become the stage for a foreign-ordered killing, and that the response, so far, is a single expelled diplomat.
