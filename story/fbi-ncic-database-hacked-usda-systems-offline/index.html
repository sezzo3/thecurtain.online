Hackers Breach the FBI's Crime Database as a Cyberattack Knocks America's Farm Payments Offline

Sezgin Baktiaya · 2026-11-21 · 5 min read

Two of America's most sensitive federal systems were hit by hackers in the same week. The FBI is investigating an apparent breach of its National Crime Information Center (NCIC) — the database police officers across the country rely on to check warrants and criminal records — while the US Department of Agriculture has spent days wrestling with a cyberattack that knocked the systems paying farmers offline. In both cases, officials have not said who was responsible, or how much was taken.

CNN first reported the FBI intrusion on Friday, citing two people briefed on the investigation, and Reuters carried the report. The bureau discovered the apparent intrusion in recent days. The NCIC connects federal, state and local law enforcement agencies through a single shared network, letting officers identify wanted individuals, verify outstanding warrants and retrieve records during investigations. The FBI has not disclosed how the attackers gained access or whether they obtained confidential records, and it has not confirmed whether the accuracy of the records was affected. A former FBI official who led investigations into cybercrime groups told CNN the greatest danger may not be theft but tampering: manipulated records could mislead officers during traffic stops and arrests. FBI Director Kash Patel appeared on Fox News on Thursday to tout the bureau's cybersecurity work — after officials had already learned of the latest incident, according to CNN.

The USDA attack unfolded over a longer stretch. Nextgov/FCW first reported on Thursday that the department's Cybersecurity and Privacy Operations Center had uncovered a security incident; an internal email sent on Monday by Chris Rottler, acting assistant chief information officer for the Farm Production and Conservation Business Center, said teams had to reset all database passwords and restart applications. The outages hit the Farm Service Agency's loan software and conservation-payment programmes, along with mapping and contracting systems, according to DTN and ABC News. On Friday, NBC News reported, citing two former USDA officials, that hackers had gained access to the system that pays farmers, and that the department had told employees about it on Thursday. Under Secretary Richard Fordyce said the protective measures had worked and no data was lost, and that most systems should be back by the close of business on Friday — but as of late Thursday, outages were still intermittent and some staff could not even connect to the VPN.

The timing could hardly be worse for farmers. The outage has dragged on for more than a week as producers try to finish enrolment in the Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage programmes for the 2026 crop year, with a deadline of 11 December. Financial tools used to move money between the department and farmers — including those tied to crop insurance and disaster relief — were disrupted, according to Nextgov/FCW and AgWeb. Lenders have been unable to check the status of guaranteed loans, and crop insurance agents have been warning policyholders to confirm any change to payment instructions by phone. NBC described the incident as the first known breach at the department initiated by an outside actor.

The NCIC intrusion is the second to hit an FBI system in recent months. In September, the digital extortion group ShinyHunters breached an FBI recruitment website and stole personal data belonging to current and former employees, according to Reuters. The two incidents land amid a wider run of disruption: in the past week, outages have affected major banks, the National Security Agency and the Federal Aviation Administration's air traffic control system. The Trump administration has insisted those glitches are unrelated and were not the result of cyberattacks — a claim that has done little to calm concern in Washington about the state of federal cybersecurity.

For now, the most basic questions remain unanswered. No group has claimed either attack, no official has named a suspect, and neither agency has said publicly whether data was stolen. The FBI has not said when the NCIC breach will be contained or whether officers should treat the database's records with extra caution in the meantime. At the USDA, technicians were still working through the weekend to restore the remaining applications. What is clear is that two agencies holding some of the government's most sensitive information — criminal records on one side, the financial lifeline of American farming on the other — were both penetrated in the same week, and the public still does not know how.
